# Research & citations: Beyond Intelligence 001, "The Thinking Machine"

## Primary study (slides 05 and 06)
**Hao-Ping (Hank) Lee, Advait Sarkar, Lev Tankelevitch, Ian Drosos, Sean Rintel, Richard Banks, Nicholas Wilson. "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking: Self-Reported Reductions in Cognitive Effort and Confidence Effects From a Survey of Knowledge Workers." CHI '25, Article 1121.** DOI 10.1145/3706598.3713778
- Page: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/
- PDF: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf (read in full)

Verbatim findings used:
- "We survey 319 knowledge workers… Participants shared 936 first-hand examples of using GenAI in work tasks."
- "examples that were reported as 'much less effort' or 'less effort' comprise 72% in Knowledge, 79% in Comprehension, 69% in Application, 72% in Analysis, 76% in Synthesis, and 55% in Evaluation."
- "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking, while higher self-confidence is associated with more critical thinking." (abstract)
- "for Knowledge and Comprehension, the effort shifts from information gathering to information verification; for Application, effort shifts from problem-solving to AI response integration; and for Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation, effort shifts from task execution to task stewardship."
- Participants self-reported critical thinking in 555 of 936 examples (59.29%).

Limitations (from §6.3, shown on slide 05): self-reported (some participants conflated "less effort using AI" with "less effort thinking"); subjective confidence may not match real expertise; English-only; skewed toward younger, tech-skilled, weekly GenAI users; tools change fast. **The study shows associations in self-reports, not a measured decline in thinking ability.**

## Background (slides 03 and 04)
- **Cognitive offloading:** Evan F. Risko & Sam J. Gilbert, "Cognitive Offloading," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20(9), 676–688 (2016). DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002. Defined as using physical action to reduce the cognitive demands of a task.
- **The historical lineage** (writing/Socrates, printing/Trithemius, calculators/arithmetic teachers, the internet) is drawn from the introduction of Lee et al. (2025). Socrates' worry about writing is in Plato's *Phaedrus* (274c–275b): writing "will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it" (Fowler translation, Loeb). Trithemius: *De laude scriptorum* (1492), in praise of hand copying.
- **Irony of automation:** Lisanne Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation," *Automatica* 19(6), 775–779 (1983), cited in Lee et al.: mechanising routine work deprives people of the practice that keeps their judgement strong for the exceptions.
